How studying figurative art and the Great Books shaped my approach to problem-solving.

I was recently corresponding with someone who is helping me think through some questions as I reorient for my next position. I enjoyed articulating my response so much that I thought I would share it with my network.

TL;DR: Software engineering is an art and a science, practical and theoretical, in the most wonderful ways.

**The Question**

What are examples of problem-solving perspectives that come from your background?

**My Answer**

The two most formative experiences that shape my problem-solving perspective are:

## 1. Studying figurative art

While one might think of studying art as something abstract and remote from everyday concerns, it is actually very practical.

Oftentimes, the only answer to how to improve is: **practice, practice, practice.**

As I try to improve my coding practice, I keep in mind a saying by Ingres:

> <i>“Not a day without a line.”</i>

Through the act of doing a thing over and over, paired with the appropriate theoretical studies, you build an understanding of the theoretical underpinnings by intuition, rather than purely theoretically.

I know from this experience that it takes years to build the balance of theory and practice that leads to being good at a thing.

## 2. Attending St. John's College, a Great Books college focused on Socratic seminars

It was an education centered around first principles and asking questions to understand the principles underlying the big ideas behind these texts.

If you don't understand an idea, what is a question you can ask that clearly expresses what you don't understand?

**Hypothesize, analyze, synthesize; do so iteratively.**

This reflects how I go about solving any problem. I always try to bracket the question until I understand enough about what I don't understand, so that I can start building back up from there.

It is interesting to what degree these two perspectives come together nicely in software engineering.

On the one hand, it is a discipline that requires a good deal of (computer) science, constantly learning, and never settling for having arrived.

On the other hand, it is immensely practical and requires a tremendous amount of practice to cement that understanding.

It is very creative in that way, constantly swinging between learning and doing in order to build systems that solve real problems.
